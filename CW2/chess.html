Basic Guide on How to Play Chess

Introduction to Chess

Chess is a two-player strategy board game played on a checkered board with 64 squares arranged in an 8x8 grid. Each player starts with 16 pieces: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and eight pawns. The goal of the game is to checkmate the opponent's king.

Setting Up the Board


Here's how to set up a standard chess board:

The board is oriented so that each player has a white (or light-colored) square in the bottom right corner.
The pieces are arranged in two rows closest to each player.
The back rank (the row closest to the player) is filled with: Rook, Knight, Bishop, Queen, King, Bishop, Knight, Rook.
The Queen always goes on the square that matches her color (white queen on white square, black queen on black square). The King goes on the remaining square on the back rank.
The row in front of the back rank is filled entirely with Pawns.
White moves first.

How the Pieces Move


Each type of chess piece moves in a specific way:

King: Moves one square in any direction (horizontally, vertically, or diagonally). The King cannot move into a square that is attacked by an opponent's piece (into 'check').
Queen: Moves any number of unoccupied squares in any direction (horizontally, vertically, or diagonally).
Rook: Moves any number of unoccupied squares horizontally or vertically.
Bishop: Moves any number of unoccupied squares diagonally. A Bishop always stays on squares of the same color it started on.
Knight: Moves in an 'L' shape: two squares in one direction (horizontally or vertically) and then one square perpendicular to that. The Knight is the only piece that can jump over other pieces.
Pawn: Moves forward one square. On its very first move, a Pawn can move forward one or two squares. Pawns capture diagonally one square forward. Pawns have special moves called 'en passant' and 'promotion'.

Pieces capture an opponent's piece by moving onto the square occupied by that piece.

Basic Rules and Concepts


Check: When a King is under immediate attack by an opponent's piece, it is in 'check'. The player whose King is in check must make a move to remove the King from attack on their next turn. This can be done by moving the King, blocking the attack with another piece, or capturing the attacking piece.
Checkmate: If a King is in check and there is no legal move to remove it from attack, it is 'checkmate'. The player whose King is checkmated loses the game.
Stalemate: If a player's King is NOT in check, but the player has no legal moves available, the game is a 'stalemate'. A stalemate results in a draw (a tie).
Draw: A game can also end in a draw by mutual agreement, repetition of moves, or insufficient material to checkmate.

Objective

The ultimate goal is to trap the opponent's King so it cannot escape being captured (checkmate).

Getting Started


The best way to learn is by playing! Find someone who knows how to play, or use an online chess platform or app that can guide you through games.

Conclusion

Chess is a challenging but rewarding game of strategy. Understanding how each piece moves and the basic rules of check and checkmate is the first step. Good luck!
